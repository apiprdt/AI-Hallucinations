\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This exploratory study provides empirical evidence that the type of hallucination---not merely its presence---is associated with differential outcomes in LLM-augmented molecular property prediction. Through a controlled nine-condition ablation using a four-category taxonomy, we observe that Structural Phantom (SP) hallucinations exhibit the strongest predictive association on the BBBP physicochemical task (ROC-AUC 0.626 vs.\ 0.533 baseline), despite being naturally underproduced by the model (approximately 2.4\% of free hallucinations). Conversely, on the BACE enzymatic task, hallucination augmentation consistently degraded performance, suggesting that augmentation utility is sensitive to the alignment between the perturbation and the task's knowledge requirements.

Our shuffled semantic control (C5) provides evidence that the observed gains on BBBP are not attributable solely to scientific writing style, as disrupting molecule-description alignment eliminates discriminative improvement while preserving linguistic register.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited scope of this pilot study: a single LLM, two datasets, and single-split evaluation. We do not propose hallucination augmentation as a practical technique, nor do we claim to have identified the causal mechanism underlying the observed effects. Rather, this work aims to provide a structured empirical characterization that can guide future investigation into when and why semantic perturbations affect LLM-based molecular reasoning.

\paragraph{Future directions.} Key extensions include: (1) multi-model validation across diverse LLM architectures, (2) larger-scale evaluation with cross-validation, (3) refinement of the CC residual category, (4) investigation of the prediction collapse phenomenon on additional enzymatic datasets, and (5) comparison with fine-tuned and traditional ML baselines to contextualize the practical significance of the observed effects.
