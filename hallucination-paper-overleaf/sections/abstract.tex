Synthetic textual augmentations generated by large language models (LLMs) have been observed to influence molecular property prediction, but the mechanisms and task-specificity of these effects remain poorly characterized. We introduce a controlled semantic perturbation framework to evaluate how the \textit{semantic orientation} of text---its topical focus and structural claims---modulates zero-shot prediction behavior in LLMs. We evaluate nine conditions across physicochemical, enzymatic, toxicity, and solubility benchmarks. Our primary finding is a robust and statistically significant task-dependent sensitivity: semantic fabrication systematically degrades performance and probability calibration on enzymatic (BACE) and solubility (ESOL) tasks. This degradation is characterized by a reproducible ``distributional compression,'' wherein the LLM's predictive confidence collapses toward a non-informative unimodal state ($D_{KL} > 14$). Conversely, on general physicochemical (BBBP) and toxicity (Tox21) tasks, prompt-induced structural topic focus (C4a) yields directional improvements in predictive discriminability (Cohen's $d = +0.51$). We demonstrate that the semantic orientation of a perturbation, rather than its factual taxonomic correctness, is the primary driver of these behavioral shifts. These findings indicate that text-conditioned LLM molecular inference is highly sensitive to task-dependent semantic conditioning, underscoring the necessity of distributional calibration analysis when deploying LLMs in scientific reasoning domains.
