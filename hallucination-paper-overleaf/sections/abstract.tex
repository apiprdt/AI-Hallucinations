\textbf{Background:} Recent studies suggest that large language model (LLM) hallucinations may influence molecular property prediction, yet the nature of this effect remains poorly characterized across different hallucination types and model scales.

\textbf{Methods:} We introduce a controlled semantic perturbation framework using a heuristic categorization scheme. We utilize three prompt-constrained perturbation operators---Structural Phantom (SP), Property Inversion (PI), Mechanism Fabrication (MF)---alongside a residual Contextual Confabulation (CC) category. We evaluate nine conditions on two MoleculeNet benchmarks (BBBP, $N=204$; BACE, $N=152$) using multi-seed evaluation (Llama-3.1-8B, $n=3$ seeds) and cross-model validation on selected conditions (C0, C3) using Llama-3.3-70B. A random-permutation control isolates semantic effects from stylistic priming.

\textbf{Results:} On BBBP, Structural Phantom (SP) hallucinations yield the largest observed ROC-AUC increase (0.626 vs.\ 0.533 baseline), consistent across 97.6\% of bootstrap resamples, though none of the BBBP improvements reached statistical significance after correction ($p > 0.05$). The 70B model exhibits reversed sensitivity (C3: 0.549 vs.\ 0.644 baseline). On BACE, all models and seeds exhibit consistent distributional compression toward chance level. The random-permutation control confirms that the observed effects are semantically grounded rather than driven by scientific register alone.

\textbf{Conclusions:} LLM-based molecular predictions show varied sensitivity to structured semantic perturbations. The observed performance shifts are directionally consistent within tasks but vary across task domain and model capacity, suggesting that semantic augmentations can introduce unpredictable inductive biases. These exploratory findings highlight the critical need for rigorous, controlled evaluation when utilizing generative augmentations in scientific reasoning pipelines.
