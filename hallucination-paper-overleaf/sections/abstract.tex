Recent work has reported that hallucinated molecular descriptions generated by large language models (LLMs) can improve downstream molecular property prediction, but the mechanisms underlying this effect remain uncharacterized. In this pilot study, we introduce a rule-based hallucination taxonomy comprising four operationally defined categories---Structural Phantom (SP), Property Inversion (PI), Mechanism Fabrication (MF), and Contextual Confabulation (CC)---and evaluate their differential effects through a nine-condition controlled ablation on two MoleculeNet classification benchmarks using Llama-3.1-8B. On the blood-brain barrier permeability task (BBBP, $N=204$), SP hallucinations yielded the highest observed ROC-AUC (0.626 vs.\ 0.533 baseline), while free hallucination produced intermediate gains (0.594). In contrast, on the BACE-1 enzyme inhibition task ($N=152$), all hallucination conditions degraded performance relative to baseline (0.656), with free hallucination reducing ROC-AUC to 0.488. A circular-shift semantic control preserved scientific register while disrupting molecule-description alignment, producing no discriminative improvement over baseline (ROC-AUC 0.530), suggesting that semantic content rather than stylistic priming drives the observed gains on BBBP. Analysis of naturally generated hallucinations revealed a distributional bias toward generic Contextual Confabulation ($\sim$80\%), despite the rarer Structural Phantom category exhibiting the strongest predictive association. These exploratory results indicate that hallucination utility in LLM-augmented molecular prediction is sensitive to both hallucination type and task domain, though the limited sample sizes and single-model evaluation warrant cautious interpretation and further validation.
