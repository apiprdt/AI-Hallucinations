Synthetic textual augmentations generated by large language models (LLMs) have been observed to influence molecular property prediction, but the task-specificity of these effects remains poorly characterized. We introduce a controlled semantic perturbation framework, using a heuristic taxonomy as a design scaffold, to evaluate how the \textit{semantic orientation} of text---its topical focus and structural claims---modulates zero-shot prediction behavior in LLMs. We evaluate nine conditions across physicochemical, enzymatic, toxicity, and solubility benchmarks. Our results reveal a task-dependent divergence in sensitivity: semantic fabrication triggers a robust and statistically significant degradation of performance and probability calibration on enzymatic (BACE) tasks, with directional consistency observed on solubility (ESOL). This degradation is characterized by a reproducible ``distributional compression,'' wherein the LLM's predictive confidence collapses toward a non-informative unimodal state ($D_{KL} > 14$). Conversely, on general physicochemical (BBBP) and toxicity (Tox21) tasks, prompt-induced structural topic focus yields directional trends in discriminative capacity (Cohen's $d = +0.51$). We demonstrate that the semantic orientation of a perturbation can drive these behavioral shifts even in the absence of factual taxonomic correctness. These findings indicate that text-conditioned LLM molecular inference is highly sensitive to task-dependent semantic conditioning, underscoring the necessity of distributional calibration analysis when deploying LLMs in chemical reasoning domains.
