Prior work has reported that hallucinated molecular descriptions can, in some settings, improve downstream prediction performance. However, the conditions under which this effect emerges remain poorly characterized. In this exploratory study, we propose a heuristic, operational taxonomy of hallucination types---Structural Phantom (SP), Property Inversion (PI), Mechanism Fabrication (MF), and Contextual Confabulation (CC)---and evaluate their impact through a nine-condition perturbation analysis on two classification benchmarks (BBBP, BACE) using Llama-3.1-8B. On the BBBP task ($N=204$), SP hallucinations yielded the highest observed ROC-AUC (0.626 vs.\ 0.533 SMILES-only baseline), representing the largest improvement among evaluated conditions. In contrast, on the BACE task ($N=152$), hallucination conditions degraded performance and exhibited pronounced distributional compression toward negative predictions. A circular-shift control preserved scientific tone while disrupting semantic alignment. This condition yielded baseline-level performance (0.530), indicating that semantic content, rather than stylistic priming, influences prediction outcomes within this evaluated setting. Analysis of unprompted hallucinations revealed a distributional bias toward CC ($\sim$80\%), despite the rare SP category exhibiting the strongest predictive association on BBBP. These findings suggest that hallucination utility is sensitive to both task domain and hallucination type. We explicitly bound these observations to the evaluated single-model, limited-sample setting, suggesting that hallucinations may not be adequately treated as a single homogeneous phenomenon.
