\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We organize this discussion by explicitly separating empirical observations from interpretive speculation, identifying alternative explanations, and establishing rigorous boundaries for our claims.

\subsection{Distributional Bias in Hallucination Generation}
\paragraph{Observation.} Across both datasets, naturally generated hallucinations (C3) are overwhelmingly classified as Contextual Confabulation (CC, $\sim$80\%), with Structural Phantom (SP) accounting for only 1.3--2.4\% of outputs. Despite this rarity, forced SP hallucinations (C4a) exhibited the strongest positive association with ROC-AUC on BBBP, though we emphasize this observation is based on a limited number of naturally occurring instances.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} We characterize this distributional asymmetry as a generative bias toward generic, scientifically plausible narratives over structurally specific confabulations. This labeling is purely descriptive and does not imply a causal generative mechanism within the LLM architecture.

\paragraph{Alternative Explanations.} The low SP rate may reflect prompt design interactions rather than an intrinsic model bias; different prompt formulations or higher temperatures might elicit more structurally specific outputs. Additionally, our rule-based classifier may conservatively undercount SP instances if the generated text employs structural synonyms not captured by our heuristic keyword dictionary.

\subsection{Task-Dependent Asymmetry}
\paragraph{Observation.} On BBBP, hallucination augmentation (C3, C4a--c) was associated with improved ROC-AUC relative to the SMILES-only baseline. Conversely, on BACE, all hallucination conditions degraded performance, with C3 reducing ROC-AUC from 0.656 to 0.488. The BACE prediction distribution exhibited pronounced compression, shifting from a bimodal pattern to a narrow unimodal distribution centered near $p \approx 0.17$.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} This task-dependent asymmetry suggests that hallucination utility is highly sensitive to the target domain. One plausible interpretation is that the LLM's pre-training corpus contains more robust latent representations of general physicochemical properties (relevant to BBBP) than specific enzymatic mechanisms (BACE), leading to constructive textual perturbation in the former and destructive interference in the latter.

\paragraph{Alternative Explanations.} We emphasize that these observed gains on BBBP do not demonstrate that hallucinations enhance genuine molecular reasoning. The improvements may partially reflect latent statistical correlations introduced by textual perturbation that happen to align with task-relevant physicochemical descriptors in the BBBP evaluation set. The BACE degradation may similarly reflect dataset-specific class imbalance interacting adversely with the hallucinated text, or differences in intrinsic molecular complexity between the two datasets. We cannot distinguish among these mechanisms with the current experimental design. However, while these results do not establish improved molecular reasoning, they provide evidence suggesting sensitivity of downstream predictions to structured semantic perturbations under the evaluated conditions.

\subsection{Semantic Content vs.\ Stylistic Priming}
\paragraph{Observation.} The circular-shift control (C5) preserved scientific register while disrupting molecule-description semantic alignment. C5 produced no discriminative improvement over baseline (ROC-AUC 0.530 vs.\ 0.533 for C0), despite utilizing text that is linguistically indistinguishable from C3 hallucinations.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} This dissociation is consistent with the hypothesis that the performance shifts observed under C3 are not attributable solely to generic scientific writing style or prompt length, but depend on some degree of semantic interaction between the description and the target molecule.

\paragraph{Boundary.} The C5 control uses a simple circular shift ($i \to i+1$). While our use of scaffold-split ordering reduces the risk of adjacent molecules sharing high structural similarity, C5 may still retain partial semantic relevance if structurally related molecules remain proximal in the dataset. Future work should employ fully randomized permutations or adversarial embeddings for stronger isolation.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
To prevent overinterpretation, we explicitly bound our claims:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We report observational associations within a controlled perturbation framework; we do not establish a causal mechanism for how hallucinations alter LLM predictions.
    \item The observed utility of structurally grounded (SP) perturbations is strictly bounded to the BBBP dataset, the tested prompt templates, and the Llama-3.1-8B model. We do not claim this represents a generalized phenomenon.
    \item We do not propose hallucination augmentation as a practical, deployable method for molecular property prediction. The observed effects may be artifacts of the zero-shot evaluation setting rather than robust indicators of reasoning capacity.
    \item The proposed heuristic taxonomy is operational, not exhaustive. The CC category is a residual class, and finer-grained semantic ontologies are required for comprehensive analysis.
    \item We note that traditional machine learning approaches, such as the Random Forest baseline reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}, are likely to achieve substantially higher predictive performance than the zero-shot LLM settings evaluated here.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, these findings highlight that LLM-based molecular prediction pipelines can exhibit non-trivial sensitivity to semantically structured perturbations, suggesting that seemingly benign textual augmentations may introduce systematic biases in downstream predictions. Understanding this sensitivity is critical for interpreting LLM-assisted predictions, particularly in settings where textual inputs are used as auxiliary signals.
