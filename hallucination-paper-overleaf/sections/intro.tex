\section{Introduction}

Molecular property prediction is a critical component of early-stage drug discovery, enabling prioritization of candidate compounds before expensive synthesis and biological assays~\cite{vamathevan2019applications}. Machine learning approaches---including graph neural networks~\cite{gilmer2017neural}, fingerprint-based classifiers~\cite{rogers2010extended}, and descriptor-based models---have become standard tools, with MoleculeNet~\cite{wu2018moleculenet} providing widely adopted benchmarks across physicochemical, enzymatic, and toxicity prediction tasks.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been applied to molecular reasoning by converting SMILES notation into natural language contexts for text-based inference~\cite{guo2024llm, ross2022large}. Within this line of work, an unexpected observation has emerged: hallucinated molecular descriptions---scientifically styled but factually incorrect text---can improve downstream prediction performance~\cite{hao2024hallucination}. This finding challenges the implicit assumption that factual correctness is a prerequisite for useful augmentation.

However, existing investigations of this phenomenon share several limitations. First, hallucination is treated as a monolithic category, obscuring potentially important differences among types of factual error. Second, prior studies lack semantic controls capable of disentangling the contribution of molecular-relevant content from generic scientific language effects. Third, it remains unclear whether hallucination-based gains generalize across prediction tasks with different underlying knowledge requirements.

This work addresses these gaps through a controlled perturbation framework rather than a model improvement or prompt engineering study. We make the following distinction explicit: we do not propose a method for improving molecular prediction, nor do we claim to explain why hallucinations affect LLM reasoning. Instead, we empirically characterize \emph{which types} of structured semantic perturbations are associated with performance changes, and under \emph{which task conditions} these associations appear.

Specifically, we introduce a four-category hallucination taxonomy---Structural Phantom (SP), Property Inversion (PI), Mechanism Fabrication (MF), and Contextual Confabulation (CC)---operationally defined through rule-based classification against RDKit-computed molecular ground truth. We evaluate this taxonomy through a nine-condition ablation framework on two MoleculeNet benchmarks representing distinct reasoning regimes: BBBP (physicochemical prediction) and BACE (enzyme inhibition prediction). Our framework incorporates both stylistic and semantic controls, including a circular-shift condition that preserves scientific register while disrupting molecule-description alignment.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible, rule-based hallucination taxonomy for molecular description augmentation, operationally grounded in computable molecular properties.
    \item A controlled ablation framework incorporating semantic and stylistic controls absent from prior evaluations of hallucination-augmented prediction.
    \item Empirical evidence that hallucination utility varies substantially across both hallucination type and task domain, with structurally grounded perturbations exhibiting the strongest association with performance gains on physicochemical tasks.
    \item Characterization of a performance collapse phenomenon on enzymatic prediction tasks, where hallucination augmentation degrades discriminative capacity.
    \item Evidence that scientific writing style alone is insufficient to account for observed performance differences, based on a controlled semantic disruption experiment.
\end{itemize}

We emphasize that this is an exploratory pilot study ($N=204$ for BBBP, $N=152$ for BACE) conducted with a single LLM (Llama-3.1-8B). The findings are intended to motivate and structure future investigation rather than to establish definitive causal mechanisms.
