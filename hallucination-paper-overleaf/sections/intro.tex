\section{Introduction}

Molecular property prediction is a critical component of early-stage drug discovery, enabling the prioritization of candidate compounds before expensive synthesis and biological assays~\cite{vamathevan2019applications}. Machine learning approaches---including graph neural networks~\cite{gilmer2017neural}, fingerprint-based classifiers~\cite{rogers2010extended}, and descriptor-based models---have become standard tools, with MoleculeNet~\cite{wu2018moleculenet} providing widely adopted benchmarks across physicochemical, enzymatic, and toxicity tasks.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been applied to molecular reasoning by converting SMILES notation into natural language contexts for text-based inference~\cite{guo2024llm, ross2022large}. Within this line of work, an unexpected observation has emerged: hallucinated molecular descriptions---scientifically styled but factually incorrect text---have been associated with improved downstream prediction performance in specific settings~\cite{hao2024hallucination}. This finding challenges the implicit assumption that strict factual correctness is a prerequisite for useful textual augmentation.

However, existing investigations of this phenomenon share several limitations. First, hallucination is typically treated as a monolithic category, obscuring potentially important differences among types of factual error. Second, prior studies often lack semantic controls capable of disentangling the contribution of molecular-relevant content from generic scientific language effects (stylistic priming). Third, it remains unclear whether these hallucination-associated performance variations generalize across prediction tasks with different underlying knowledge requirements.

This work addresses these gaps through a controlled perturbation framework. We make the following scoping explicit: we do not propose a novel architecture for molecular prediction, nor do we claim to mechanistically explain why hallucinations alter LLM reasoning. Instead, we empirically characterize \emph{which types} of structured semantic perturbations are associated with performance shifts, and under \emph{which task conditions} these associations emerge.

Specifically, we introduce a four-category hallucination taxonomy---Structural Phantom (SP), Property Inversion (PI), Mechanism Fabrication (MF), and Contextual Confabulation (CC)---operationally defined through rule-based classification against RDKit-computed ground truth. We evaluate this taxonomy through a nine-condition ablation on two MoleculeNet benchmarks representing distinct regimes: BBBP (physicochemical) and BACE (enzyme inhibition). Our framework incorporates both stylistic and semantic controls, notably a circular-shift condition that preserves scientific register while disrupting molecule-description alignment.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible, rule-based taxonomy for molecular description augmentation, operationally grounded in computable properties.
    \item A controlled ablation framework incorporating semantic and stylistic controls absent from prior exploratory evaluations.
    \item Empirical evidence that hallucination utility varies substantially across both hallucination type and task domain, with structurally grounded perturbations (SP) exhibiting the strongest positive association with physicochemical prediction.
    \item Characterization of a performance collapse phenomenon on enzymatic prediction, where hallucination augmentation induces pronounced distributional compression and degrades discriminative capacity.
    \item Evidence suggesting that scientific writing style alone is insufficient to account for observed performance differences, based on a controlled semantic disruption experiment.
\end{itemize}

We explicitly bound this exploratory pilot study ($N=204$ for BBBP, $N=152$ for BACE) to a single LLM evaluation (Llama-3.1-8B). These findings are intended to structure future empirical investigation into LLM augmentation rather than to establish definitive causal mechanisms.
