\section{Introduction}

Molecular property prediction is a critical component of early-stage drug discovery, enabling the prioritization of candidate compounds before expensive synthesis and biological assays~\cite{vamathevan2019applications}. Machine learning approaches---including graph neural networks~\cite{gilmer2017neural}, fingerprint-based classifiers~\cite{rogers2010extended}, and descriptor-based models---have become standard tools, with MoleculeNet~\cite{wu2018moleculenet} providing widely adopted benchmarks across physicochemical, enzymatic, and toxicity tasks.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been applied to molecular reasoning by converting SMILES notation into natural language contexts for text-based inference~\cite{guo2024llm, ross2022large}. Within this line of work, an observation has been reported in prior work: hallucinated molecular descriptions---scientifically styled but factually incorrect text---have been associated with improved downstream prediction performance in specific settings~\cite{hao2024hallucination}. This finding is in tension with the common assumption that strict factual correctness is a prerequisite for useful textual augmentation.

However, existing investigations of this phenomenon share several limitations. First, hallucination is typically treated as a monolithic category, obscuring potentially important differences among types of factual error. Second, prior studies often lack semantic controls capable of disentangling the contribution of molecular-relevant content from generic scientific language effects (stylistic priming). Third, it remains unclear whether these hallucination-associated performance variations generalize across prediction tasks with different underlying knowledge requirements. Understanding this sensitivity is important for interpreting LLM-augmented predictions, particularly in settings where textual augmentation may introduce uncontrolled semantic perturbations.

This work addresses these gaps through a controlled perturbation framework. We make the following scoping explicit: we do not propose a novel architecture for molecular prediction, nor do we claim to mechanistically explain why hallucinations alter LLM reasoning. Instead, we empirically characterize \emph{which types} of structured semantic perturbations are associated with performance shifts, and under \emph{which task conditions} these associations emerge.

The primary contribution of this work is the introduction of a controlled semantic perturbation framework to systematically evaluate how structured hallucination types influence LLM-based molecular prediction. We define a lightweight, operational categorization scheme—comprising Structural Phantom (SP), Property Inversion (PI), Mechanism Fabrication (MF), and Contextual Confabulation (CC) types—utilized as controlled perturbation operators to isolate specific semantic effects. We evaluate this framework through a nine-condition ablation on two MoleculeNet benchmarks representing distinct regimes: BBBP (physicochemical) and BACE (enzyme inhibition).

Our key contributions include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A controlled perturbation framework enabling the systematic isolation of hallucination-associated performance variations through semantic and stylistic disruption.
    \item A lightweight categorization scheme for molecular description errors, operationally defined as perturbation operators to maintain interpretability and reproducibility.
    \item Evidence of a task-dependent divergence in hallucination sensitivity, where structurally grounded (SP) perturbations are associated with directional improvements in physicochemical prediction, while similar augmentations induce performance degradation in enzymatic tasks.
    \item Characterization of prediction distribution compression on BACE, where hallucination augmentation shifts bimodal baseline distributions toward non-discriminative narrow peaks.
    \item A random-permutation control experiment demonstrating that semantic content, rather than stylistic register alone, is the primary driver of observed performance shifts within the evaluated setting.
    \item Evidence of architecture-dependent hallucination sensitivity, where smaller models exhibit performance gains under free hallucination augmentation on physicochemical tasks, while larger models show reversed sensitivity, suggesting a non-linear relationship between model capacity and perturbation response.
\end{itemize}

We explicitly bound this exploratory pilot study ($N=204$ for BBBP, $N=152$ for BACE) to two LLM scales. These findings are intended to structure future empirical investigation into LLM augmentation rather than to establish definitive causal mechanisms.
